# BHRT Clinic vs Primary Care: Who Should Prescribe Your Hormones?

You finally worked up the courage to bring up your symptoms — the crushing fatigue, the brain fog that makes you feel like a stranger in your own life, the sleep that stopped working — and your doctor handed you an antidepressant and a pamphlet. If that sounds familiar, you are not alone, and you are not imagining things. The question of BHRT clinic vs primary care is one of the most consequential choices you will make on your hormone health journey, and the answer is more nuanced than most people expect.

This post breaks down exactly how these two care settings differ, what each does well, where each falls short, and how to figure out which type of provider is actually equipped to help *you* — based on your symptoms, your history, and what you want your life to look like on the other side of treatment.

## What Makes BHRT Prescribing Different From Standard Medical Care

BHRT prescribing is a fundamentally different clinical discipline from managing blood pressure or treating an infection. The goal is not to restore a single marker to a "normal" range — it is to calibrate an interconnected hormonal system to a level where a specific person feels well, and then hold it there through an ongoing process of testing, adjustment, and monitoring.

That distinction matters enormously when choosing a provider. Standard primary care is designed around episodic, problem-oriented visits. A typical appointment runs 15–20 minutes and is built to address one or two acute concerns. Hormone optimization, by contrast, requires time: time to take a detailed symptom history, time to interpret a comprehensive lab panel, and time — sometimes months of it — to dial in the right dose and delivery method for a patient whose needs are unique.

This is not a criticism of primary care physicians, who are often brilliant clinicians working within a system that does not give them the time or reimbursement structure to do hormone optimization well. It is simply a structural reality. Many primary care doctors are also candid about it: they received limited training in hormone therapy during residency, and the field has evolved substantially since then. The 2002 Women's Health Initiative study — now significantly reinterpreted — scared many conventionally trained physicians away from prescribing estrogen for years, and some never returned to it.

The good news is that excellent BHRT care exists in multiple settings. Your job is to find a provider — wherever they practice — who has the training, the tools, and the commitment to actually optimize your hormones, not just check a box.

## How Primary Care Doctors Approach Hormone Therapy

Primary care doctors can prescribe BHRT, and some do it very well. A family physician or internist who has invested in continuing education around menopause and hormone optimization — particularly through the North American Menopause Society (NAMS) or similar bodies — can be an outstanding BHRT provider, especially for straightforward cases.

Where primary care tends to fall short is in the specifics of implementation. Research and clinical observation consistently point to a few recurring gaps:

**Testing depth.** Many primary care offices order a minimal hormone panel — often just FSH, estradiol, and thyroid-stimulating hormone — rather than the comprehensive baseline that includes free and total testosterone, DHEA-S, progesterone, SHBG, and cortisol. Without this full picture, it is easy to miss root causes or underdose.

**Delivery method familiarity.** Primary care physicians most commonly prescribe oral or patch-based conventional HRT. They may have limited experience with compounded formulations, subcutaneous pellets, or topical creams — the delivery methods most associated with bioidentical hormone protocols.

**Follow-up structure.** Dose titration requires multiple follow-up labs over the first several months. In a busy primary care practice, this can fall through the cracks, leaving patients stuck on an initial dose that is not working.

**Willingness to treat symptoms.** Perhaps most frustratingly for patients: some primary care doctors will only treat hormones if lab values fall outside a "normal" reference range, even when the patient feels terrible within that range. Symptom-based dosing, which many BHRT specialists use, requires a philosophy shift that not every generalist has made.

None of this means you should give up on your primary care doctor. It means you should ask the right questions before assuming they are or are not the right fit.

## How Dedicated BHRT Clinics Operate Differently

A dedicated BHRT clinic — whether it is branded as a hormone optimization center, a functional medicine practice, a longevity clinic, or an integrative wellness practice — is built from the ground up around a single discipline. That focus changes everything about the patient experience.

The intake process at most hormone clinics begins with a comprehensive symptom questionnaire and a broad baseline lab panel. Before you see the provider for your first consultation, your numbers are already in hand. The appointment itself tends to be longer — 45 to 90 minutes is common — and the conversation covers not just lab values but sleep quality, stress load, sexual health, cognitive function, and long-term goals.

Hormone clinics typically maintain familiarity with the full spectrum of delivery methods: oral, transdermal patches, topical creams and gels, sublingual troches, vaginal preparations, and subcutaneous pellets. They are more likely to work with compounding pharmacies that prepare individualized formulations. They are also structured for ongoing follow-up: most build a 4–6 week check-in and a 3-month re-test into the standard protocol as a matter of routine.

The trade-off is cost and insurance. Many dedicated hormone clinics operate as cash-pay or membership-based practices, meaning insurance reimbursement is limited or nonexistent. Out-of-pocket costs can run from $200–$500 for an initial consultation and $100–$300 or more per follow-up, before accounting for lab costs. For some patients, this is prohibitive. For others, it is the price of finally being heard and helped.

Quality varies significantly across hormone clinics, too. The space has attracted some providers who prioritize volume over individualization. A reputable clinic will never prescribe without labs. If any provider offers to prescribe hormones based on a symptom questionnaire alone, walk away.

## The Specialist Middle Ground: OB-GYNs and Endocrinologists

Between primary care generalists and dedicated hormone clinics sits a middle tier worth considering: OB-GYNs and endocrinologists who have made hormone therapy a focus of their practice.

A gynecologist who has completed NAMS menopause practitioner certification, or who actively manages perimenopause and menopause in a significant portion of their patient population, often brings deep clinical experience with hormone prescribing alongside the diagnostic credibility of a specialist. They are also more likely to be covered by insurance, at least for the consultation and lab components.

Endocrinologists specialize in the hormonal system by definition — but their focus is often on pathological conditions like thyroid disease, diabetes, and adrenal disorders rather than hormone optimization in otherwise healthy patients experiencing age-related decline. Some endocrinologists are excellent BHRT prescribers; others have limited interest in or familiarity with the nuances of optimizing estrogen, progesterone, and testosterone in symptomatic perimenopausal women or men with low-normal testosterone.

The key question to ask any specialist — just as with primary care — is the same: *How many patients are you currently managing on BHRT, and what does your follow-up protocol look like?* The answer will tell you more than the credential on the door.

## Red Flags and Green Flags When Evaluating Any BHRT Provider

Every provider setting has a quality spectrum. These signals apply whether you are sitting in a primary care office or a hormone optimization clinic.

**Green flags — signs of a qualified BHRT prescriber:**
- Orders comprehensive baseline labs before any prescription
- Discusses multiple delivery method options and explains the trade-offs
- Schedules follow-up labs at defined intervals (typically 4–8 weeks after initiation, then every 3–6 months)
- Acknowledges that dose adjustment takes time and frames the first 3 months as a titration period
- Discusses benefits *and* risks transparently, including updated data from post-WHI research
- Is willing to adjust the protocol if you are not feeling well, rather than dismissing your symptoms

**Red flags — signs to look elsewhere:**
- Offers to prescribe based on symptoms alone, without labs
- Uses a single "one-size-fits-all" hormone panel or protocol for every patient
- Cannot explain why they are recommending a specific delivery method for your case
- Dismisses your symptoms as "just aging" or refuses to consider a hormone-related cause
- Has no structured follow-up schedule after initiation
- Pressures you into an annual membership or expensive supplement bundles as a condition of care

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can my primary care doctor prescribe BHRT?

Yes, some primary care doctors do prescribe BHRT, but many are not trained in compounded or bioidentical hormone protocols and may decline or refer you out. Your best outcome depends less on the provider's title and more on their specific training, comfort level with hormone optimization, and willingness to do comprehensive lab testing and follow-up dosing adjustments.

### What kind of doctor specializes in hormone therapy?

Gynecologists, endocrinologists, and integrative or functional medicine physicians are the most common specialists who prescribe BHRT. Dedicated hormone clinics — staffed by any of these specialists or trained nurse practitioners — often provide the most focused care because hormone optimization is their entire practice focus, not one concern among hundreds.

### Is a hormone clinic better than a regular doctor for BHRT?

For most people seeking BHRT, a dedicated hormone clinic offers advantages: more comprehensive baseline testing, familiarity with a wider range of delivery methods, and structured follow-up protocols designed specifically for dose titration. That said, a well-trained primary care doctor or OB-GYN who stays current on hormone therapy guidelines can deliver excellent care at a lower cost.

### How do I know if a BHRT provider is qualified?

Look for providers who order a full hormone panel before prescribing — not just TSH or a single estradiol reading. Ask whether they are certified through organizations like the American Academy of Anti-Aging Medicine (A4M) or have completed menopause training through NAMS. A qualified provider will discuss multiple delivery options, explain risks transparently, and schedule follow-up labs at defined intervals.

## Common Questions About Choosing a BHRT Provider

### Who is the best doctor to see for hormone replacement therapy?

The best doctor for hormone replacement therapy is one who orders comprehensive baseline labs, offers multiple delivery method options, and schedules structured follow-up appointments to adjust your dose. This may be a dedicated hormone clinic, a NAMS-certified OB-GYN, a functional medicine physician, or — less commonly but possible — a primary care doctor with advanced hormone training.

### Can a nurse practitioner prescribe bioidentical hormones?

Yes, in most U.S. states, nurse practitioners with prescriptive authority can and do prescribe BHRT. Many hormone clinics are staffed primarily by NPs or physician assistants who work under physician oversight. What matters is their specific training in hormone optimization and the quality of the clinic's testing and follow-up protocols — not whether they hold an MD.

### Why won't my doctor prescribe BHRT?

Many doctors decline to prescribe BHRT because of limited training in hormone optimization, lingering concerns from the 2002 Women's Health Initiative study, or institutional guidelines that discourage compounded formulations. It is not always a reflection of your symptoms being invalid — it often reflects a gap in their training or scope. Seeking a second opinion from a provider who specializes in hormone therapy is a reasonable and common next step.

### How much does it cost to see a hormone specialist?

Initial consultations at dedicated hormone clinics typically range from $200 to $500 out of pocket, with follow-up visits running $100 to $300. Lab costs can add $100 to $400 depending on the panel and whether your insurance covers it. OB-GYN and primary care visits for hormone management are more likely to be partially covered by insurance, though the scope of care may be narrower.

### What labs should a BHRT provider order before prescribing?

A thorough BHRT baseline panel typically includes estradiol, FSH, LH, free and total testosterone, progesterone, DHEA-S, SHBG, thyroid panel (TSH, free T3, free T4), and a complete metabolic panel. Some providers also include cortisol, vitamin D, and a lipid panel. If a provider wants to prescribe based on a symptom survey alone — without any labs — that is a significant red flag.
