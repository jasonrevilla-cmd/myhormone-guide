# Men's Hormone Testing: What to Ask Your Doctor

You've been feeling off for months — maybe longer. Fatigue that doesn't lift after a full night of sleep, a brain that used to be sharp now running in slow motion, a body composition that seems to shift no matter what you do in the gym. You bring it up at your annual physical, and your doctor orders a testosterone test. The result comes back "within normal range," and you're sent home with nothing. Men's hormone testing, done right, involves a lot more than a single number — and knowing what to ask can change everything.

This guide breaks down the complete male hormone panel blood test: which markers actually matter, what the numbers mean in context, what time of day to get tested, and the exact questions to bring to your next appointment. Whether you're early in your search or have already been dismissed once, this is the information you deserve to have.

## Why Most Men's Hormone Testing Falls Short

Standard testosterone testing for men at a routine physical typically measures only total testosterone — and that single marker misses the majority of the clinical picture. Total testosterone measures all the testosterone in your bloodstream, but a significant portion of it is bound to proteins and therefore unavailable for your body to use. What your tissues actually respond to is free testosterone — the small fraction circulating unbound and biologically active.

Here's where it gets clinically important: a man can have a total testosterone reading that sits comfortably within the lab's "normal" range — say, 420 ng/dL — while his free testosterone is effectively low because a protein called sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) is elevated, binding up most of his available hormone. He'll feel every symptom of low testosterone (you can run through the [full checklist for men here](/low-testosterone-symptoms-the-full-checklist-for-men/)), but his chart says he's fine.

This is not a rare edge case. Research published in the *Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism* has consistently shown that the relationship between total testosterone, SHBG, and free testosterone is nonlinear and highly individual. A provider who orders only total testosterone is, in effect, looking at one piece of a five-piece puzzle and calling it complete.

## The Complete Male Hormone Panel Blood Test: Every Marker That Matters

A comprehensive male hormone panel blood test should cover five interconnected systems: the androgen axis, the estrogen axis, the adrenal axis, the pituitary signals, and thyroid function. Here is what each marker reveals.

**Androgen Axis**
- **Total Testosterone** — The baseline starting point. Useful for context, but never interpret it alone.
- **Free Testosterone** — The biologically active fraction. Low free T with normal total T explains many "within normal range" men who feel terrible.
- **SHBG (Sex Hormone-Binding Globulin)** — Elevated SHBG reduces free testosterone availability. It tends to rise with age, certain medications, and liver conditions.
- **DHT (Dihydrotestosterone)** — A potent androgen converted from testosterone; relevant for men considering therapy or dealing with hair loss concerns.

**Estrogen Axis**
- **Estradiol (E2)** — Men produce and need estrogen, but too much — often from testosterone converting via the aromatase enzyme — causes symptoms like water retention, mood instability, and reduced libido. Optimal estradiol for men is typically 20–30 pg/mL.

**Pituitary Signals**
- **LH (Luteinizing Hormone)** — Signals the testes to produce testosterone. High LH with low testosterone suggests primary hypogonadism (testicular dysfunction). Low LH with low testosterone suggests a pituitary or hypothalamic issue.
- **FSH (Follicle-Stimulating Hormone)** — Relevant for men concerned about fertility, and useful for differentiating the type of hypogonadism present.

**Adrenal Axis**
- **DHEA-S (Dehydroepiandrosterone Sulfate)** — A precursor hormone produced by the adrenal glands that declines sharply with age. Low DHEA-S is associated with fatigue, mood changes, and reduced resilience to stress.
- **Cortisol (morning draw)** — Chronically elevated cortisol suppresses testosterone production. Understanding adrenal function puts the hormonal picture in context.

**Thyroid Function**
- **TSH, Free T3, Free T4** — Thyroid dysfunction mimics and compounds low testosterone symptoms almost perfectly. Many men who feel "off" have both suboptimal testosterone and undiagnosed thyroid dysfunction.

**Metabolic Context**
- **CBC (Complete Blood Count)** — Baseline before starting any therapy; monitors red blood cell count (important on TRT).
- **Comprehensive Metabolic Panel** — Liver and kidney function, which affect hormone metabolism.
- **PSA (Prostate-Specific Antigen)** — Baseline is recommended before initiating testosterone therapy, per Endocrine Society guidelines.
- **Lipid Panel** — Hormones and cardiovascular health are tightly linked.

## What Time to Test — and Why It Changes Everything

Morning testosterone testing, ideally between 7:00 and 10:00 a.m., is essential for accurate men's hormone testing. Testosterone follows a circadian rhythm with its peak in the early morning hours. An afternoon blood draw can read 20–25% lower than a morning draw in the same man on the same day — a difference large enough to change whether he qualifies for treatment.

Similarly, fasting for 9–12 hours before the draw matters. Food — particularly carbohydrate-heavy meals — temporarily suppresses testosterone levels. Many men who have been told their levels are fine were tested mid-afternoon after lunch, a scenario that can produce a falsely low or deceptively normal result depending on timing and individual response.

**Practical checklist for test day:**
- Schedule the appointment for first thing in the morning (7–10 a.m.)
- Fast overnight — water is fine
- Avoid intense exercise the day before (acute heavy training temporarily elevates cortisol and can suppress testosterone)
- Avoid alcohol for 48 hours prior
- Disclose any supplements — even zinc, vitamin D, or ashwagandha — as they can affect readings

If your previous testosterone test was done at any other time of day or without fasting, that result may not be reliable. Asking your doctor to retest under the correct conditions is entirely reasonable.

## How to Talk to Your Doctor: Questions That Get Results

Many men find this part the most frustrating. You've done your research, you know something is wrong, and yet the conversation at the appointment can feel like hitting a wall. Here are specific, evidence-grounded questions to bring.

**Ask for the full panel, not just total testosterone:**
> "I'd like a complete male hormone panel that includes free testosterone, SHBG, estradiol, LH, FSH, DHEA-S, morning cortisol, thyroid markers, CBC, CMP, and PSA. Can we order those together?"

**Ask for your actual numbers, not just "normal":**
> "Can I see the exact values and reference ranges? I'd like to understand where I fall within the range, not just whether I'm technically inside it."

**Challenge an "afternoon" or "random" draw:**
> "I've read that testosterone levels are highest in the morning and drop significantly by afternoon. Can we schedule this for a morning fasting draw?"

**Ask about free testosterone specifically:**
> "My total testosterone is within range, but I'm still having significant symptoms. Can we look at free testosterone and SHBG together to understand how much is actually available to my tissues?"

**Ask about treatment options if warranted:**
> "If my free testosterone is low, what are my options? I'd like to understand both TRT and bioidentical approaches." (For a side-by-side breakdown of those two paths, see [TRT vs. BHRT for Men: What's the Difference?](/trt-vs-bhrt-for-men-whats-the-difference/).)

If your current provider won't order a full panel or dismisses your symptoms without explanation, seeking a second opinion from an endocrinologist or a hormone specialist is a legitimate and often necessary step.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What hormones should men get tested?

A complete male hormone panel should include total testosterone, free testosterone, SHBG, estradiol, LH, FSH, DHEA-S, thyroid hormones (TSH, Free T3, Free T4), and cortisol. Many providers also add a comprehensive metabolic panel and CBC. Testing this full set gives a far more accurate picture of hormonal health than total testosterone alone, which is what most standard checkups include.

### What is a normal testosterone level for men?

Most laboratories define the normal range for total testosterone in adult men as roughly 300–1,000 ng/dL, though reference ranges vary slightly by lab. However, "normal" on paper doesn't mean optimal. Many men report significant low-testosterone symptoms at levels between 300–450 ng/dL. Free testosterone and SHBG context matters as much as the total number.

### Should I fast before a testosterone blood test?

Yes — fasting for 9–12 hours before testosterone testing is generally recommended, and testing should be done in the morning between 7–10 a.m. Testosterone levels follow a circadian rhythm and are highest in the morning. An afternoon draw can read 20–25% lower than a morning draw, potentially leading to a misdiagnosis or an incorrect treatment decision.

### Can a regular doctor order a male hormone panel blood test?

Yes, your primary care physician or internist can order a male hormone panel blood test. However, many men find that their GP orders only total testosterone and stops there if it falls within range. Asking specifically for free testosterone, SHBG, estradiol, and thyroid markers — or seeking a referral to an endocrinologist or a hormone specialist — usually produces a more complete workup.

## Common Questions About Men's Hormone Testing

### How do I know if my testosterone is too low?

Symptoms like persistent fatigue, low libido, brain fog, mood changes, and difficulty building muscle despite regular exercise are common signals. The only way to confirm is through a blood test — ideally a morning fasting draw that includes both total and free testosterone, not just one marker. Many men with low-normal total testosterone still feel significant symptoms because their free testosterone is inadequate.

### Can I test my hormones at home without going to a doctor?

At-home hormone test kits are available and can measure testosterone through saliva or finger-prick blood samples. They can be a useful starting point for understanding trends, but they are generally less accurate than a venipuncture blood draw analyzed at a clinical lab. Most hormone specialists will want a standard serum blood panel before making any clinical recommendations.

### Why does my doctor say my testosterone is normal if I still feel terrible?

"Normal" lab ranges are built from population averages and don't account for individual optimal levels. A value of 320 ng/dL is technically within the reference range but may be far below what your body functions best at. Additionally, if only total testosterone was tested, free testosterone — the fraction your cells actually use — may be low even when the total number looks acceptable.

### How often should men get their hormones tested?

For men not on therapy, annual testing is a reasonable baseline once symptoms appear or after age 45. For men already on hormone therapy, most specialists retest every 3–6 months during the dose-adjustment period, then move to every 6–12 months once levels are stable. Retesting timing and frequency should always be guided by your provider based on your specific protocol.

### Does insurance cover a full male hormone panel blood test?

Coverage varies widely by plan and provider. Total testosterone testing is commonly covered when a physician documents symptoms consistent with hypogonadism. Additional markers like free testosterone, SHBG, and DHEA-S may require prior authorization or may be billed separately. Calling your insurance's member services line with the specific CPT codes your provider plans to use is the most reliable way to confirm coverage before your appointment.
